1What is Shear?

Shear refers to a situation where a force acts parallel or tangential to a surface, causing one layer of a material to move or slide relative to another layer.

In simple words: shear means sliding layers past each other.

Did you know?

The word "shear" comes from the Old English sceran — "to cut". Sheep are shorn by sliding blades parallel to the skin. Same idea: layers sliding past each other.

Each card slides a little more than the one below it. The layers have moved relative to one another — that type of deformation is called shear deformation.

2Shear Force vs Normal Force

A shear force is a force that acts parallel to a surface and tends to make one part of a material slide relative to another part.

Simulation · Direction of Force Left: force into the surface · Right: force along the surface

Normal force — acts perpendicular to the surface → produces compression / stretching.

Shear force — acts parallel to the surface → produces sliding.

Did you know?

Skyscrapers must resist both kinds of force. Gravity pushes straight down (normal), while wind shears sideways. Engineers design for both — that's why tall buildings have deep foundations and stiff bracing.

The force is acting along the surface rather than directly into it. That single difference changes everything about how the material responds.

3Shear Stress

When a shear force acts over an area, we define shear stress as the force per unit area:

τ = FA τ = shear stress · F = shear force · A = area over which the force acts
Pa = N / m² The SI unit of shear stress is the pascal

So: shear force is the actual force, while shear stress is the force per unit area.

Did you know?

Earthquakes are giant shear events. Two blocks of crust slide past each other along a fault — the same sliding motion as the orange block in the simulation above, just hundreds of kilometres long and decades in the making.

4Now — What is a Fluid?

A fluid is a substance that deforms continuously when subjected to shear stress.

Liquids wateroilpetrolmilk
Gases airoxygensteam

The important word is:

C O N T I N U O U S L Y

Not "deforms a bit". Not "deforms and stops". It keeps going for as long as the shear stress is applied.

Did you know?

Old windows are not thicker at the bottom because glass flows. That's a myth — glass is an amorphous solid, not a fluid. Old glass was simply made unevenly and glaziers installed the thicker side down.

5Why Does Shear Cause a Fluid to Flow?

Imagine water between two plates — the top plate moving, the bottom plate fixed.

Did you know?

Isaac Newton was the first to write down this linear relation: τ = μ · du/dy. Fluids that obey it are called Newtonian. Water, air, and oil are Newtonian — but ketchup, blood, and paint are not. Their viscosity changes with shear rate.

Because the top plate moves, the fluid layers also move relative to each other. The layers near the moving plate move faster; the layers near the fixed plate move more slowly. The fluid is undergoing shear deformation — and it never stops while the plate keeps moving.

The velocity profile is linear: u(y) = U · y/h, so the shear rate du/dy = U/h is constant throughout the gap, and τ = μ·U/h is the same at every height.

6What Happens If We Keep Applying Shear?

This is the key difference between a solid and a fluid.

Solid — shear stress applied → solid deforms → finite deformation → it can remain in that deformed state.

Fluid — shear stress applied → fluid begins to deform → layers slide → deformation continues → flow continues.

Fluid + shear stress → continuous deformation → flow

The relative movement of the layers does not stop as long as the shear stress continues to act. That is what continuous deformation means.

Did you know?

The Pitch Drop Experiment at the University of Queensland started in 1927. Pitch looks like a brittle solid you can shatter with a hammer — yet it slowly drips through a funnel. In almost 100 years, it has dripped only nine times. Proof that "continuous" can mean "one drop per decade".

Important clarification

"A solid can sustain shear stress" does not mean a solid cannot deform. A solid may deform:

  • elastically → returns to its original shape
  • plastically → permanent deformation remains

The important distinction is that a solid can sustain a finite deformation, whereas a fluid under sustained shear stress keeps deforming.

Did you know?

Metals can also flow — just very slowly. Red-hot steel squeezed through a rolling mill deforms like thick dough. So "solid vs fluid" isn't only about the material: it's also about the timescale and the temperature.

9Exam-Friendly Definitions

Shear
Shear is the relative sliding of adjacent layers of a material due to a force acting parallel to the surface.
Shear Force
Shear force is a force acting parallel or tangential to a surface, tending to cause relative sliding between adjacent layers.
Shear Stress
τ = F / A  — shear stress is the shear force acting per unit area. Unit: Pa = N/m².
Fluid
A fluid is a substance that deforms continuously under the action of shear stress.

Shear → layers slide

Fluid + shear stress → continuous sliding → flow

Did you know?

One pascal is roughly the pressure of a single sheet of paper lying flat on a table. So a shear stress of a few hundred pascals is already quite large — the units are small on purpose, like pence versus pounds.
