Why a fluid sticks to a wall, why its own molecules pull on each other, and how that turns into the no-slip condition, a velocity profile and a boundary layer.

τ = μ · du/dy  →  no-slip → profile → boundary layer

1What is Adhesion?

Adhesion is the attraction between different substances.

For example:

water ↔ glass water ↔ metal oil ↔ solid surface

Easy way to remember it: Adhesion = different.

Simulation · Water on Glass Dashed dark links = fluid–solid interaction
Water molecule Glass surface atom Adhesion bond
What to notice: the water molecules closest to the glass are pulled downwards towards the surface atoms — that is the adhesion bond. Slide the strength to 0 and the dark dashed links disappear: with no fluid–solid attraction the molecules no longer cling to the glass. The grey fluid–fluid links stay, because those are a different interaction.

Water molecules near the glass are pulled toward it. That pull is the fluid–solid interaction we call adhesion. Slide the strength to zero and the links fade away.

2What is Cohesion?

Cohesion is the attraction between molecules of the same substance.

For example:

water ↔ water oil ↔ oil air ↔ air

Easy way to remember it: Cohesion = same.

Simulation · Water Molecules Grey links = fluid–fluid interaction
Water molecule Cohesion bond
What to notice: there is no wall here — every molecule is surrounded by its own kind. Increase the cohesion strength and the grey links become thick and dark: the molecules pull on each other and the whole group holds together as one body of liquid. Reduce it to 0 and the links fade — the molecules would drift apart like a gas. This internal “stickiness” is what lets neighbouring fluid layers drag on one another.
Adhesion = different   ·   Cohesion = same Two words, two different interactions

3Adhesion vs Cohesion

Consider water flowing along a solid wall. Two different interactions are happening at the same time.

Simulation · Both Interactions at Once Toggle each bond type on and off
Adhesion — water ↔ wall (different) Cohesion — water ↔ water (same)
What to notice: switch off adhesion and the bottom row of molecules lifts away from the wall — the fluid no longer sticks to the surface. Switch off cohesion and the molecules stop pulling on each other — the fluid would lose its internal grip. Real fluids have both at the same time, and each one produces a different macroscopic effect (next page).

4Why Are They Important?

These two interactions help us understand what happens when a fluid touches a solid wall, and what happens between neighbouring regions inside the fluid.

Be careful with the wording

It is not correct to say:

  • “Adhesion is viscosity.”
  • “Cohesion is viscosity.”

They are related concepts, but they are not the same thing. Molecular interactions contribute to viscous behaviour; viscosity is the macroscopic property we use to quantify it.

5What is Viscosity?

Viscosity describes a fluid's resistance to relative motion between neighbouring fluid regions.

Water flows easily. Honey flows slowly. Honey has a higher viscosity than water.

6Does Cohesion Cause Viscosity?

This is where we need to be precise. The simple answer is:

Fluid–fluid molecular interactions contribute to viscous behaviour.

Cohesive interactions are part of the molecular interactions inside a fluid, and those interactions allow momentum transfer between neighbouring regions.

Fluid–fluid molecular interactions
↓
Momentum transfer
↓
Resistance to relative motion
↓
Viscous behaviour

Cohesion ≠ viscosity

Viscosity is the macroscopic property used to quantify the resistance.

For a Newtonian fluid, that quantification is written as:

τ = μ dudy τ = shear stress · μ = dynamic viscosity · du/dy = velocity gradient

7The Newtonian Viscosity Equation

τ = μ dudy Shear stress = viscosity × velocity gradient

This one equation is the heart of viscous flow. To really understand it, we need a situation where we can see every term. That situation is Couette flow.

Couette flow — the simplest viscous flow

Picture a thin layer of fluid sandwiched between two flat plates:

  • The bottom plate is fixed. The fluid touching it must obey the no-slip condition, so u = 0 there.
  • The top plate slides sideways at speed U. The fluid touching it must move at u = U.
  • Between the two plates, every fluid layer is dragged along by the layer above it and held back by the layer below it.

Because the fluid is Newtonian and the geometry is simple, the velocity profile turns out to be a straight line:

u(y) = U · yh Linear velocity profile between the plates

Since the profile is a straight line, its slope is the same at every height:

dudy = Uh = constant Shear rate is uniform across the gap

And because the shear rate is constant, the shear stress is constant too:

τ = μ Uh = constant Every layer feels exactly the same shear stress

Try these three experiments

  • Set U = 0. The top plate stops. Notice the profile collapses, the shear rate becomes zero, and the shear stress vanishes. No relative motion → no viscous stress.
  • Halve h. The shear rate doubles. Same plate speed, but the fluid is squeezed into a thinner gap, so layers must slide past each other faster. τ doubles too.
  • Increase μ. The profile shape does not change — because it is set by geometry and plate speed, not by viscosity. But τ rises: the fluid resists the same deformation more strongly.

Key insight: the profile shape is set by the geometry and the boundary conditions. The shear stress is set by the viscosity and the shear rate. In Couette flow both are uniform across the gap.

8What Happens Near a Wall?

Now consider fluid flowing over a stationary wall. The fluid directly touching the wall behaves very differently from the fluid farther away.

The wall's influence does not stop the whole fluid. It simply decreases with distance from the wall. That decreasing influence is exactly what lets a velocity profile exist.

9The No-Slip Condition

ufluid at wall = uwall The velocity of the fluid at the wall equals the velocity of the wall

This is one of the most important boundary conditions in viscous flow. Notice it does not say u = 0 — it says the fluid matches the wall.

When u_wall = 0 the profile leans over and τ is at its largest. When u_wall = U∞ the profile becomes perfectly flat, du/dy = 0 and the shear stress disappears — that is rigid-body motion, not shear.

Stationary wall: uwall = 0 → the fluid at the wall has zero velocity. It does not mean the whole fluid is stationary.

Moving wall: uwall = U → the fluid at the wall moves at U too.

Special case: when uwall = U∞, the entire fluid moves at the same speed → the profile is flat → du/dy = 0 → no shear stress.

10No-Slip Inside a Pipe

Exactly the same rule applies inside a pipe. The thin layer of fluid touching the pipe wall sticks to the wall, so its velocity must equal the wall velocity — which is zero for a fixed pipe.

ufluid at wall = uwall = 0 No-slip at both walls of the pipe

Because the fluid is held still at the walls but must carry a finite flow rate, the velocity has to be largest in the middle and zero at the edges. For slow (laminar) flow this gives the familiar parabolic profile:

u(r) = umax · ( 1 − (r/R)² ) r = distance from the centre-line · R = pipe radius

Important: if there were no no-slip, the fluid would slide along the wall like a frictionless plug and there would be no velocity gradient at the wall, no wall shear stress and no need for a pressure gradient to drive the flow. Every real pipe-flow calculation — friction factor, head loss, pumping power — starts from the no-slip condition.

What to notice: the dashed line is the tangent to the velocity profile where it meets the wall. Its slope is exactly (du/dy)wall. Increase U∞ and the tangent gets steeper — τw rises. Increase μ at fixed U∞ and the tangent does not change, but τw still rises because τw = μ·(du/dy). The row of dark arrows at the bottom is the equal-and-opposite force the fluid exerts on the wall — skin-friction drag.

Skin-friction drag

When fluid flows over a solid surface, viscous effects create a tangential force between the fluid and the surface. The fluid and the wall exert equal and opposite tangential forces on each other — the wall feels a resisting force called skin-friction drag.

Increase either μ or (du/dy)wall and the wall shear stress rises — and so does the skin-friction drag.

13The Boundary Layer

The boundary layer is the region of fluid close to a solid surface where viscous effects are important and the velocity changes from the wall velocity toward the free-stream velocity.

What to notice: the shaded wedge is the boundary layer. It is zero thickness at the leading edge and grows downstream because the wall's retarding influence has had more time (and more distance) to diffuse outward. The small velocity profiles drawn at five stations show the same story: at every station u = 0 on the plate and u ≈ U∞ at the top of the wedge. Raise U∞ and the wedge gets thinner; raise ν and it gets thicker.

At the wall u = 0. Outside the boundary layer u ≈ U∞. Somewhere in between the velocity must change between those two values — that region is the boundary layer.

Higher U∞ → thinner boundary layer. Higher viscosity → thicker boundary layer.

For a stationary wall, u = 0 at the surface, but u → U∞ far from the wall. Therefore there must be a region where the velocity transitions between those two values. That region is the boundary layer.

15The People-Holding-Hands Analogy

Imagine a vertical column of people standing on a moving walkway (the wall). The person at the bottom is gripping the walkway; each person above is holding hands with the one below.

Because the walkway drags the bottom person, and each person drags the one above, the column leans over — exactly the way a velocity profile leans over.

What to notice:
  • The people are stacked vertically, one above the other — each one represents a different fluid layer at a different distance y from the wall.
  • The dark dashed link at the bottom is the wall–person grip (adhesion). The grey dashed links between people are the person–person grips (cohesion / internal friction).
  • With wall speed = 0, person 1 is held still and each person above drifts further right — the column leans over. That lean is the velocity profile, and the speed difference between neighbours is du/dy.
  • Push the wall speed up to 1.00: now everybody moves at the same speed and the column stays perfectly upright and simply translates. That is the flat profile → du/dy = 0 → no viscous stress.

Important limitation

Do not imagine: “if the first molecule stops, every molecule must stop.” That is not what happens.

The wall's influence decreases with distance from the wall. Fluid molecules are not literally holding hands — but neighbouring fluid regions really do interact and transfer momentum.

17Exam-Friendly Definitions

Adhesion
Adhesion is the attraction or interaction between different substances — for example, between a fluid and a solid wall.
Cohesion
Cohesion is the attraction or interaction between molecules of the same substance — for example, between neighbouring water molecules.
Viscosity
Viscosity is a fluid's resistance to relative motion between neighbouring fluid regions. For a Newtonian fluid, τ = μ (du/dy).
No-Slip Condition
The velocity of the fluid at a solid boundary equals the velocity of that boundary: ufluid at wall = uwall. Inside a fixed pipe this means u = 0 at the pipe wall.
Velocity Profile
The velocity profile is the variation of fluid velocity with distance from the wall: u = u(y). In a laminar pipe it is parabolic: u = umax(1 − (r/R)²).
Velocity Gradient
du/dy — the rate at which velocity changes with distance normal to the wall.
Wall Shear Stress
τw = μ (du/dy)wall — the viscous shear stress evaluated at the wall. It is associated with skin-friction drag.
Boundary Layer
The region of fluid close to a solid surface where viscous effects are important and the velocity changes from the wall velocity to the free-stream velocity.
